# Bayesian Inference[¶](#bayesian-inference "Link to this Section")

In this section, we’ll focus on computing and using posterior distributions in more sophisticated Bayesian models. We’ll start by discussing why posterior distributions are useful in Bayesian inference, and then explain why they’re hard. Then, in the next section, we’ll learn about approximating distributions using sampling.

## Why we need posterior distributions[¶](#why-we-need-posterior-distributions "Link to this Section")

In general, we need the posterior distribution so that we can make statements and decisions about our unknown quantity of interest, <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ. We saw that for simple models like the product review model or the model for heights, it was easy to compute the posterior exactly, because we chose a conjugate prior.

In the product review example:

*   Our parameter of interest <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ represents the probability of a positive review.

*   If we chose a Beta prior, i.e., <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi><mo>∼</mo><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">B</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">e</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">t</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">a</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>α</mi><mo separator="true">,</mo><mi>β</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)</annotation></semantics>θ∼Beta(α,β), then the posterior distribution also belonged to the Beta family: <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>x</mi><mo>∼</mo><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">B</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">e</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">t</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">a</mi></mrow><mrow><mo fence="true">(</mo><mi>α</mi><mo>+</mo><mo>∑</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo separator="true">,</mo><mi>β</mi><mo>+</mo><mi>n</mi><mo>−</mo><mo>∑</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo fence="true">)</mo></mrow></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta | x \sim \mathrm{Beta}\left(\alpha + \sum x_i, \beta + n - \sum x_i\right)</annotation></semantics>θ∣x∼Beta(α+∑xi​,β+n−∑xi​).

*   This made it easy to determine things like the MAP estimate or LMSE estimate, simply by using known properties of the Beta distribution.

But what if our posterior distribution didn’t have such a convenient form? In that case, we would have to compute the posterior (and any estimates from it) ourselves:

<semantics><mtable rowspacing="0.25em" columnalign="right left" columnspacing="0em"><mtr><mtd><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mstyle></mtd><mtd><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><mrow><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mfrac></mrow></mstyle></mtd></mtr><mtr><mtd><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><mrow><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mrow><mo>∫</mo><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mi>d</mi><mi>θ</mi></mrow></mfrac></mrow></mstyle></mtd></mtr></mtable><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\begin{align} p(\theta|x) &= \frac{p(x|\theta)p(\theta)}{p(x)} \\ &= \frac{p(x|\theta)p(\theta)}{\int p(x|\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta} \\ \end{align}</annotation></semantics>p(θ∣x)​=p(x)p(x∣θ)p(θ)​=∫p(x∣θ)p(θ)dθp(x∣θ)p(θ)​​​[(1)](#gydV4BSpve "Link to this Equation")

In general, the integral in the denominator could be impossible to compute. We call the denominator the **normalizing constant**: it’s a constant because it doesn’t depend on <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ, and it’s normalizing because we need it for the distribution or density to sum or integrate to 1.

In the next section, we’ll see a few examples that illustrate why computing the normalizing constant is hard, but first, let’s look at three examples of why we might need to know it in the first place.

### Computing probabilities[¶](#computing-probabilities "Link to this Section")

Suppose we want to know the probability that <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ is greater than 0.7, given the observed data. In this case, we can set up an integral to compute this:

In the last step, we used the fact that p(x) doesn’t depend on <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ.

If we don’t know <semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p(x)</annotation></semantics>p(x), then our probability will be off by an unknown factor. For example, suppose the true probability is 0.9, the integral is 0.0009, and the normally-unknown denominator <semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p(x)</annotation></semantics>p(x) is 0.001. In this case, if we don’t know the normalizing constant, there’s no way we can determine the probability: we’ll always be wrong by an unknown factor, which means that our answer is useless.

### MAP Estimation[¶](#map-estimation "Link to this Section")

Suppose we want to compute the MAP estimate:

<semantics><mtable rowspacing="0.25em" columnalign="right left" columnspacing="0em"><mtr><mtd><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>θ</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mrow><mi>M</mi><mi>A</mi><mi>P</mi></mrow></msub></mstyle></mtd><mtd><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><mrow><mo>=</mo><mi><munder><mo><mi mathvariant="normal">argmax</mi><mo>⁡</mo></mo><mi>θ</mi></munder></mi><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mstyle></mtd></mtr><mtr><mtd><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><mrow><mo>=</mo><mi><munder><mo><mi mathvariant="normal">argmax</mi><mo>⁡</mo></mo><mi>θ</mi></munder></mi><mfrac><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mfrac></mrow></mstyle></mtd></mtr><mtr><mtd><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><mrow><mo>=</mo><mi><munder><mo><mi mathvariant="normal">argmax</mi><mo>⁡</mo></mo><mi>θ</mi></munder></mi><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mstyle></mtd></mtr></mtable><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\begin{align} \hat{\theta}_{MAP} &= \underset{\theta}{\operatorname{argmax}} p(\theta|x) \\ &= \underset{\theta}{\operatorname{argmax}} \frac{p(x|\theta)p(\theta)}{p(x)} \\ &= \underset{\theta}{\operatorname{argmax}} p(x|\theta)p(\theta) \\ \end{align}</annotation></semantics>θ^MAP​​=θargmax​p(θ∣x)=θargmax​p(x)p(x∣θ)p(θ)​=θargmax​p(x∣θ)p(θ)​​[(3)](#rPJLURgfb7 "Link to this Equation")

In the last step, we used the fact that p(x) doesn’t depend on <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ.

If <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ is low-dimensional and continuous, we can often optimize this either analytically or sometimes numerically. If <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ is discrete and doesn’t take on too many different values, we can search over all possible values. However, if <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ is discrete and takes on an intractably large number of possible values, then we’d need to search over all of them, which would be impossible. Similarly, if <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ is high-dimensional, then the search can also be intractable.

To summarize: for low-dimensional continuous variables, or discrete random variables with a low number of possible values, we can compute the MAP estimate without needing to know the exact posterior. For higher-dimensional random variables and/or discrete random variables with many possible values, this won’t work.

In order to compute the LMSE estimate, we need to compute the denominator, <semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p(x)</annotation></semantics>p(x). The same is true for computing the expected value of any other function of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ, or any other probability involving the posterior distribution. ## Why computing posterior distributions is hard[¶](#why-computing-posterior-distributions-is-hard "Link to this Section")

In simple models like our product review model or our model for heights, it was easy to compute the exact posterior for the unknown variable that we were interested in. This happened because we chose a conjugate prior. In most other cases, computing the exact posterior is hard! Here are two examples:

### One-dimensional non-conjugate prior[¶](#one-dimensional-non-conjugate-prior "Link to this Section")

Let’s return to the product review example, but this time, instead of a Beta prior, we choose <semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mn>2</mn><mi>π</mi></mfrac><mi>cos</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo fence="true">(</mo><mfrac><mi>π</mi><mn>2</mn></mfrac><mi>θ</mi><mo fence="true">)</mo></mrow></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p(\theta) = \frac{2}{\pi}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} \theta\right)</annotation></semantics>p(θ)=π2​cos(2π​θ) for <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi><mo>∈</mo><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mn>0</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mn>1</mn><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta \in [0, 1]</annotation></semantics>θ∈[0,1].

This distribution looks much more complicated: we can’t reduce it to a known distribution at all. So, in order to properly compute <semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p(\theta|x)</annotation></semantics>p(θ∣x), we’d need to figure out the normalizing constant. This requires solving the integral:

This integral is difficult to solve in closed form. In this specific example, since this is a one-dimensional problem, we could take advantage of numerical integration. As we saw above, we don’t need the normalizing constant if we’re only interested in the MAP estimate, but we can’t compute the LMSE estimate without it.
