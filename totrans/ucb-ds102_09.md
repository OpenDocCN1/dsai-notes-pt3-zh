We’ll begin our exploration of Bayesian and frequentist modeling with a simple example that highlights some key differences between the two approaches. Recall our setup: we observe some data <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo separator="true">,</mo><mo>…</mo><mo separator="true">,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>n</mi></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x_1, \ldots, x_n</annotation></semantics>x1​,…,xn​, and we’ll use them to estimate some parameter <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ of interest. In this and the following sections, we’ll focus on two simple examples to help us build intuition:

*   **Estimating product quality**: <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo separator="true">,</mo><mo>…</mo><mo separator="true">,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>n</mi></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x_1, \ldots, x_n</annotation></semantics>x1​,…,xn​ are the reviews (👍/1 or 👎/0) for a product being sold online, and <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ is a number between 0 and 1 that represents the probability of someone leaving a good review. Intuitively, <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ represents our estimate of the quality of the product.

*   **Estimating average height**: <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo separator="true">,</mo><mo>…</mo><mo separator="true">,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>n</mi></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x_1, \ldots, x_n</annotation></semantics>x1​,…,xn​ are the heights of <semantics><mrow><mi>n</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">n</annotation></semantics>n individuals in a random sample, and <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ is the average height of individuals in the population.

We’ll start with the first example.

## Intuition and computation[¶](#intuition-and-computation "Link to this Section")

Suppose you’re shopping for a new microwave. You find two choices, both of which cost about the same amount:

*   Microwave A has three positive reviews and no negative reviews

*   Microwave B has 19 positive reviews and 1 negative review

Which one should you buy?

Intuitively, there isn’t necessarily a right or wrong answer. But, in a scenario like this, most shoppers would probably prefer microwave B. Seeing more data points, even if one is negative, reassures us that the results we’re seeing in the reviews are consistent.

We’ll approach this question computationally from a frequentist and then a Bayesian perspective, and see how well each one can incorporate the intuition above.

When formulating a statistical model (regardless of choosing a Bayesian or frequentist approach), it’s important to choose a model so that the parameter we’re estimating satisfies the following criteria:

*   The parameter should reflect the underlying quantity that we’re interested in estimating. In this case, we’ll quantify how “good” each microwave is using <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ, and then we can just pick the microwave that’s “better” (i.e., has a higher value of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ). Our choice above, the probability of leaving a positive review, satisfies this, since better products should be more likely to get positive reviews than worse products.

*   The parameter should fit clearly within our probability model: in other words, our probability model should relate the observed data and the parameter. We’ll see more about how this works shortly.

Our goal will be to estimate and draw conclusions about the parameter <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ based on the observed data <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo separator="true">,</mo><mo>…</mo><mo separator="true">,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>n</mi></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x_1, \ldots, x_n</annotation></semantics>x1​,…,xn​.

## Frequentist parameter estimation: maximum likelihood[¶](#frequentist-parameter-estimation-maximum-likelihood "Link to this Section")

Before we can actually do any parameter estimation, we need to first specify the relevant probability distributions. Recall that in the frequentist setting, we’re assuming that the data (our binary reviews, <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo separator="true">,</mo><mo>…</mo><mo separator="true">,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>n</mi></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x_1, \ldots, x_n</annotation></semantics>x1​,…,xn​) are random, but that the parameter (the probability of generating a positive review <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ) is fixed and unknown.

To set up a probability model in the frequentist setting, all we need is a **likelihood** <semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p(x_i|\theta)</annotation></semantics>p(xi​∣θ). This tells us how likely a data point is, given a certain value of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ. Since our data are binary and our parameter is a number between 0 and 1, a natural choice is a **Bernoulli distribution** (for more on Bernoulli distributions, see the [Data 140 textbook](http://prob140.org/textbook/content/Chapter_03/02_Distributions.html#named-distributions) or [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_distribution)):

<semantics><mrow><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>θ</mi><mo>∼</mo><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">B</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">e</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">r</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">n</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">o</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">u</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">l</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">l</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">i</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x_i | \theta \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\theta)</annotation></semantics>xi​∣θ∼Bernoulli(θ)[(1)](#BIhxXQDa3R "Link to this Equation")

This notation means “conditioned on <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ, <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>i</mi></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x_i</annotation></semantics>xi​ follows a Bernoulli distribution with parameter <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ”. In other words:

While the underlying probability should be familiar to you, there are some important conceptual leaps that we’ve taken:

*   This notation represents a **parametric family** of distributions: different values of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ lead to different distributions for <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>i</mi></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x_i</annotation></semantics>xi​, but they all fall within the Bernoulli family of distributions.

*   Since our goal in this process is to find a “good” value of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ using the observed data <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>i</mi></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x_i</annotation></semantics>xi​, we can view this as a function of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ: for any value of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ that we consider, we can plug in our data and get the probability of observing it, if that value of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ were true.

The simplest way to estimate <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ in the frequentist setting is to use **maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)** and choose the value of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ that maximizes the likelihood: in other words, we’ll pick the value of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ that makes our data look as likely as possible.

Here’s how we’ll go about it:

1.  We’ll write the likelihood for all the data points, <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo separator="true">,</mo><mo>…</mo><mo separator="true">,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>n</mi></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x_1, \ldots, x_n</annotation></semantics>x1​,…,xn​ (we already did it for a single point above)

2.  We’ll use the log-likelihood instead of the likelihood. This will make things a little easier computationally. Why is this okay? Because <semantics><mrow><mi>log</mi><mo>⁡</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\log</annotation></semantics>log is a monotonically increasing function, so the same value of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ that maximizes <semantics><mrow><mi>log</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\log(p(x|\theta))</annotation></semantics>log(p(x∣θ)) also maximizes <semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p(x|\theta)</annotation></semantics>p(x∣θ).

3.  To find the best value of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ, we’ll take the derivative of the log-likelihood with respect to <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ, set it equal to 0, and solve.

4.  We’ll confirm that step 3 gave us a maximum (rather than a minimum) by computing the second derivative and confirming that it’s negative, and checking the boundaries.

## A different way: Bayesian inference[¶](#a-different-way-bayesian-inference "Link to this Section")

In the Bayesian setting, we’ll assume that the unknown parameter <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ is a random variable. Now, instead of looking for a single estimate, we’ll consider the probability distribution for <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ after observing the data <semantics><mrow><mi>x</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x</annotation></semantics>x. This is the conditional distribution <semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p(\theta|x)</annotation></semantics>p(θ∣x), which we call the **posterior distribution**. Throughout the rest of this chapter, we’ll learn how to compute and interpret this distribution. It represents our understanding of the randomness in <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ after seeing the data <semantics><mrow><mi>x</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x</annotation></semantics>x. We’ll compute it using Bayes’ rule:

### Choosing a prior: Beta distributions[¶](#choosing-a-prior-beta-distributions "Link to this Section")

Let’s return to our product review example. For our prior <semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p(\theta)</annotation></semantics>p(θ), which represents our belief about the unknown product quality <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ before seeing any data, we’ll choose the [beta distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_distribution). This isn’t the only distribution we can choose, but it happens to be a particularly convenient choice: we’ll see why later. Try it yourself: without doing any computation, what is the expectation of a beta-distributed random variable? What is the mode?

#### Point estimates[¶](#point-estimates "Link to this Section")

Once we have a posterior distribution, we will often want to answer the question: how do we use the distribution to get a single estimate for <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ? This single number is typically called a **point estimate**.

We’ll start with two answers for question 1: the **Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) estimate** and the **Minimum/Least Mean Squared Error (MMSE/LMSE) Estimate**.

The **MAP estimate** is the estimate that maximizes the posterior distribution. Remember that the posterior distribution represents our belief about the unknown parameter <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ. Intuitively, the MAP estimate is the <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ value that is most likely according to that belief. For a Beta<semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>α</mi><mo separator="true">,</mo><mi>β</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">(\alpha, \beta)</annotation></semantics>(α,β) distribution, we can look up the maximum value (mode) of the distribution to find that it’s <semantics><mrow><mfrac><mrow><mi>α</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><mrow><mi>α</mi><mo>+</mo><mi>β</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></mfrac></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+\beta-2}</annotation></semantics>α+β−2α−1​.

**Optional exercise**: derive the mode of the beta distribution. *Hint: instead of maximizing the Beta density with respect to <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ, try taking the log first, just like we did before.*

The **LMSE estimate** is simply the mean of the posterior distribution. It has this name because we can show that the mean of the posterior distribution minimizes the mean squared error:

#### Choosing the prior parameters[¶](#choosing-the-prior-parameters "Link to this Section")

Returning to our microwave example, we can see that our choice of prior encodes our beliefs about products and reviews.

For example, if we think that most products are good until proven otherwise by the review data, then we could choose a more “optimistic” prior, such as Beta<semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>5</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mn>1</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">(5, 1)</annotation></semantics>(5,1), which favors (assigns higher probability to) larger values of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ. In this case:

*   The posterior for the quality of microwave A is Beta<semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>8</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mn>1</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">(8, 1)</annotation></semantics>(8,1), and the MAP estimate is 1.

*   The posterior for the quality of microwave B is Beta<semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>24</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mn>2</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">(24, 2)</annotation></semantics>(24,2), and the MAP estimate is 0.96. Under this prior, microwave A looks better.

But, if we believe that most products are bad until proven otherwise by the review data, we may choose a Beta<semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>1</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mn>5</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">(1, 5)</annotation></semantics>(1,5) prior, which favors smaller values of <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ. In this case:

*   The posterior for the quality of microwave A is Beta<semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>4</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mn>5</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">(4, 5)</annotation></semantics>(4,5): if we compute the MAP estimate, we get 0.43.

*   The posterior for the quality of microwave B is Beta<semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>20</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mn>6</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">(20, 6)</annotation></semantics>(20,6), and the MAP estimate is 0.79. So, under this prior, microwave B looks better.

From this, we can take away that **our choice of prior can often have a significant effect on our results**. So, it’s important to choose priors that capture your assumptions and belief about the world, and to be explicit about communicating those assumptions with others when sharing your results.

Now suppose we instead take a Bayesian approach, and treat <semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta</annotation></semantics>θ as random. Just like before, we’re interested in the posterior distribution:

<semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo separator="true">,</mo><mo>…</mo><mo separator="true">,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>n</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo separator="true">,</mo><mo>…</mo><mo separator="true">,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>n</mi></msub><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mrow><mi>p</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo separator="true">,</mo><mo>…</mo><mo separator="true">,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>n</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mfrac></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p(\theta|x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \frac{p(x_1, \ldots, x_n|\theta)p(\theta)}{p(x_1, \ldots, x_n)}</annotation></semantics>p(θ∣x1​,…,xn​)=p(x1​,…,xn​)p(x1​,…,xn​∣θ)p(θ)​[(24)](#MBsIIt7f1j "Link to this Equation")

<semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi><mo>∼</mo><mi mathvariant="script">N</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>μ</mi><mn>0</mn></msub><mo separator="true">,</mo><msubsup><mi>σ</mi><mn>0</mn><mn>2</mn></msubsup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)</annotation></semantics>θ∼N(μ0​,σ02​)[(25)](#JQDR20vH6K "Link to this Equation")
