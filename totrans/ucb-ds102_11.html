Graphical Models, Probability Distributions, and Independence¶

Graphical Models¶

A graphical model provides a visual representation of a Bayesian hierarchical model using. These models are sometimes known as Bayesian networks, or Bayes nets.

We represent each random variable with a node (circle), and a directed edge (arrow) between two random variables indicates that the the distribution for the child variable is conditioned on the parent variable. When drawing graphical models, we usually start with the variables that don’t depend on any others. These are usually, but not always, unobserved parameters of interest like θ\thetaθ in this example. Then, we proceed by drawing a node for each variable that depends on those, and so on. Variables that are observed are shaded in.

We’ll draw graphical models for the three examples we’ve seen in previous sections: the product review model, the kidney cancer model, and the exoplanet model.

In our product review model, we have the following random variables:

xi∣θ∼Bernoulli(θ)θ∼Beta(a,b)\begin{align} x_i | \theta &\sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\theta) \\ \theta &\sim \mathrm{Beta}(a, b) \end{align}xi​∣θθ​∼Bernoulli(θ)∼Beta(a,b)​​

Relating graphical models to probability distributions¶

When we were drawing graphical models above, we drew one node per variable, and started from the “top,” working with the variables that didn’t depend on any others. We then worked our way through the model, ending with observed variables. When looking at a graphical model to derive the corresponding joint distribution of all the variables in the model, we follow a similar process. For example, in the kidney cancer death rate model, we can write the joint distribution of all the variables in our model by starting at the root (i.e., the nodes that have no parents), and then proceeding through their children, writing the joint distribution as a product.

So, we start with p(a)p(a)p(a) and p(b)p(b)p(b), then p(θi∣a,b)p(\theta_i | a, b)p(θi​∣a,b) (for i∈{1,…,C}i \in \{1, \ldots, C\}i∈{1,…,C}), then p(yi∣θi)p(y_i | \theta_i)p(yi​∣θi​):

p(a,b,θ1,…,θC,y1,…,yC)=p(a)p(b)∏i=1Cp(θi∣a,b)p(yi∣θi)p(a, b, \theta_1, \ldots, \theta_C, y_1, \ldots, y_C) = p(a)p(b) \prod_{i=1}^C p(\theta_i\mid a, b) p(y_i\mid\theta_i)p(a,b,θ1​,…,θC​,y1​,…,yC​)=p(a)p(b)i=1∏C​p(θi​∣a,b)p(yi​∣θi​)
(3)

Factoring the distribution this way helps us understand and mathematically demonstrate the independence and dependence relationships in our graphical models, as we’ll see shortly.

Independence and Conditional Independence¶

Review: independence and conditional independence¶

We say that two random variables www and vvv are independent if knowing the value of one tells us nothing about the distribution of the other. Notationally, we write w⊥ ⁣ ⁣ ⁣⊥vw \perp\!\!\!\perp vw⊥⊥v. The following statements are all true for independent random variables www and vvv:

  • If www and vvv are independent (w⊥ ⁣ ⁣ ⁣⊥vw \perp\!\!\!\perp vw⊥⊥v), then the joint distribution p(w,v)p(w, v)p(w,v) can be written as the product of the marginal distributions: p(w,v)=p(w)p(v)p(w, v) = p(w)p(v)p(w,v)=p(w)p(v).

  • If www and vvv are independent (w⊥ ⁣ ⁣ ⁣⊥vw \perp\!\!\!\perp vw⊥⊥v), then the conditional distributions are equal to the marginal distributions: p(w∣v)=p(w)p(w|v) = p(w)p(w∣v)=p(w) and p(v∣w)=p(v)p(v|w) = p(v)p(v∣w)=p(v).

Exercise: using the definition of conditional distributions, show that the two conditions above are mathematically equivalent.

We say that two random variables www and vvv are conditionally independent given a third random variable uuu if, when we condition on uuu, knowing the value of one of vvv or www tells us nothing about the distribution of the other. Notationally, we write w⊥ ⁣ ⁣ ⁣⊥v∣uw \perp\!\!\!\perp v \mid uw⊥⊥v∣u, and mathematically this means that p(w,v∣u)=p(w∣u)p(v∣u)p(w, v \mid u) = p(w\mid u) p(v \mid u)p(w,v∣u)=p(w∣u)p(v∣u).

For example, suppose x1x_1x1​ and x2x_2x2​ are the heights of two people randomly sampled from a very specific population with some average height μ\muμ: this population could be college students, or second-graders, or Olympic swimmers, or some other group entirely.

If we know the value of μ\muμ, then x1x_1x1​ and x2x_2x2​ are conditionally independent, because they’re random samples from the same distribution with known mean μ\muμ. For example, if we are given that μ=4′1′′\mu = 4'1''μ=4′1′′, then knowing x1x_1x1​ does not tell us anything about x2x_2x2​.

Suppose instead that we don’t know the value of μ\muμ. Then, we find out that x1=7′1′′x_1 = 7' 1''x1​=7′1′′. In this case, we might guess that the ‘specific population’ is likely a very tall group, such as NBA players. This will affect our belief about the distribution of x2x_2x2​ (i.e., we should expect the second person to be tall too). So, in this case:

  • x1x_1x1​ and x2x_2x2​ are conditionally independent given μ\muμ: x1⊥ ⁣ ⁣ ⁣⊥x2∣μx_1 \perp\!\!\!\perp x_2 \mid \mux1​⊥⊥x2​∣μ.

  • x1x_1x1​ and x2x_2x2​ are not unconditionally independent: it is not true that x1⊥ ⁣ ⁣ ⁣⊥x2x_1 \perp\!\!\!\perp x_2x1​⊥⊥x2​.

Independence and conditional independence in graphical models¶

The structure of a graphical model can tell us a lot about the independence relationships between the variables in our model. Specifically, we can determine whether two random variables are unconditionally independent or conditionally independent given a third variable, just by looking at the structure of the model. Let’s look at a few examples to illustrate this. We’ll start with the height example we just saw:

From our reasoning above, we know that x1⊥ ⁣ ⁣ ⁣⊥x2∣μx_1 \perp\!\!\!\perp x_2 \mid \mux1​⊥⊥x2​∣μ, but that x1x_1x1​ and x2x_2x2​ are not unconditionally independent. This is true in general for any three variables in a graphical model in this configuration.

Exercise: mathematically prove the results stated above.

Solution: To show that x1x_1x1​ and x2x_2x2​ are not unconditionally independent, we must show that p(x1,x2)≠p(x1)p(x2)p(x_1, x_2) \neq p(x_1)p(x_2)p(x1​,x2​)=p(x1​)p(x2​). We can compute p(x1,x2)p(x_1, x_2)p(x1​,x2​) by looking at the joint distribution over all three variables and then marginalizing over μ\muμ:

p(x1,x2)=∫p(x1,x2,μ)dμ=∫p(μ)p(x1∣μ)p(x2∣μ)dμ\begin{align*} p(x_1, x_2) &= \int p(x_1, x_2, \mu) d\mu \\ &= \int p(\mu) p(x_1 \mid \mu) p(x_2 \mid \mu) d\mu \end{align*}p(x1​,x2​)​=∫p(x1​,x2​,μ)dμ=∫p(μ)p(x1​∣μ)p(x2​∣μ)dμ​
(4)

Unfortunately, there is no way to factor the integral that separates terms with x1x_1x1​ and terms with x2x_2x2​, so this does not factor. In other words, in general, the integral above will not equal p(x1)p(x2)p(x_1)p(x_2)p(x1​)p(x2​), so the variables are not unconditionally independent.

What about conditional independence given μ\muμ? We need to show that p(x1,x2∣μ)=p(x1∣μ)p(x2∣μ)p(x_1, x_2\mid\mu) = p(x_1\mid\mu) p(x_2\mid\mu)p(x1​,x2​∣μ)=p(x1​∣μ)p(x2​∣μ):

p(x1,x2∣μ)=p(x1,x2,μ)p(μ)=p(μ)p(x1∣μ)p(x2∣μ)p(μ)=p(x1∣μ)p(x2∣μ)\begin{align*} p(x_1, x_2 \mid \mu) &= \frac{p(x_1, x_2, \mu)}{p(\mu)} \\ &= \frac{p(\mu) p(x_1 \mid \mu) p(x_2 \mid \mu)}{p(\mu)} \\ &= p(x_1 \mid \mu) p(x_2 \mid \mu) \end{align*}p(x1​,x2​∣μ)​=p(μ)p(x1​,x2​,μ)​=p(μ)p(μ)p(x1​∣μ)p(x2​∣μ)​=p(x1​∣μ)p(x2​∣μ)​
(5)

This mathematical result aligns with the intuition we built in the previous section.

Let’s look at another example:

In this example, xxx and zzz are not unconditionally independent. Intuitively, we can see that yyy depends on xxx, and zzz depends on yyy, so that xxx and zzz are dependent.

But, xxx and zzz are conditionally independent given yyy: the lack of an arrow directly from xxx to zzz tells us that zzz only depends on xxx through yyy.

Let’s look at a third example:

In this example, xxx and zzz are unconditionally independent, but given yyy, they are conditionally dependent. Why? Let’s look at an example that will help us build intuition for this result. Suppose that:

  • yyy is whether or not I have a stuffy nose.

  • xxx is whether or not I am sick (with a cold, flu, COVID, etc.)

  • zzz is whether or not I have seasonal allergies.

First, we can see that the description matches the graphical model: whether or not I have a stuffy nose depends on whether or not I’m sick, and whether or not I have allergies. But, sickness and allergies don’t affect each other. In other words, if I don’t know anything about whether I have a stuffy nose, then my sickness and allergies are independent of each other.

Now, suppose I wake up one morning with a stuffy nose (i.e., y=1y=1y=1), and I’m trying to determine whether I’m sick or have allergies. I look at the weather forecast, and see that the pollen counts are very high. As soon as I hear this information, I’m a lot more certain that z=1z=1z=1. But, even though the weather forecast didn’t directly tell me anything about whether or not I’m sick, my belief that I’m sick drops significantly: my symptoms have been explained away by the explanation that I probably have allergies.

In other words, conditioned on a value of yyy (stuffy nose), knowing something about zzz (allergies) gives me information about the distribution of xxx (sickness). This is precisely the definition of conditional dependence.

Exercise: mathematically prove the results above.
