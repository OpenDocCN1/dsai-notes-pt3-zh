Statistical Decision Theory

原文：https://data102.org/ds-102-book/content/chapters/01/decision-theory

Up until now, we’ve used error rates to help us understand tradeoffs in binary decision-making. In this section, we’ll introduce a more general theoretical framework to understand and quantify errors we make, and start to explore the theoretical branch of statistics known as statistical decision theory.

Remember our setup: we have some unknown quantity θ\thetaθ that we’re interested in. We collect data xxx. Our data are random, and come from the distribution p(x∣θ)p(x|\theta)p(x∣θ). We use the data to reason about θ\thetaθ. Often, we’ll want to use the data to compute an estimate for θ\thetaθ but sometimes, we may want to do something slightly different. In order to describe “the thing we do to the data”, we’ll use the notation δ(x)\delta(x)δ(x). This represents the result of applying some procedure δ\deltaδ to the data. For example, δ\deltaδ might be the sample average of many data points, or the result of logistic regression. The obvious next question is: how do we choose which procedure δ\deltaδ to use? We’ll decide by quantifying how “good” each δ\deltaδ is, and then trying to choose the “best” one.

“Good” is a very abstract notion: to quantify it, we’ll need a quantitative measure of how good (or to be more precise, how bad) our procedure δ\deltaδ is. We’ll call this a loss function. Notationally, we’ll write ℓ(θ,δ(x))\ell(\theta, \delta(x))ℓ(θ,δ(x)) to represent the loss associated with the outcome δ(x)\delta(x)δ(x) if the true value is θ\thetaθ. To summarize:

Continuous decision: ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​ loss¶

Now, suppose our parameterθ\thetaθ is continuous, and δ(x)\delta(x)δ(x) is our estimate of the parameter from the data. To make things a little more concrete, θ\thetaθ could be the average height of people in a population, and xxx could be the heights of people in a random sample from that population. In this case, our loss shouldn’t just be right vs wrong: we should use a loss function that’s smaller when our estimate is close, and larger when our estimate is far away.

Known and unknown¶

At this point, you may be wondering: if θ\thetaθ is unknown, how can we ever compute the loss function? It’s important to keep in mind that when we apply δ(x)\delta(x)δ(x) on real data, we don’t know θ\thetaθ. But right now, we’re building up some machinery to help us analyze different procedures. In other words, we’re trying to get to a place where we can answer questions like “what procedures are most likely to give us estimates that are close to θ\thetaθ?”

Fixed and random: finding the average loss in Bayesian and frequentist approaches¶

The loss function is a function of δ(x)\delta(x)δ(x), which is the procedure result for particular data xxx, and the particular parameter θ\thetaθ. This isn’t particularly useful to us: we’d like to understand how the loss does “on average”. But in order to compute any kind of averages, we need to decide what’s random and what’s fixed. This is an important fork in the road: we can either take the Bayesian or the frequentist route. Let’s examine what happens if we try each one:

Frequentist loss analysis¶

In the frequentist world, we assume that our unknown θ\thetaθ is fixed. The data are the only random piece. So, we’re going to look at the average across different possibilities for the data xxx. Since the data comes from the distribution p(x∣θ)p(x|\theta)p(x∣θ), which depends on θ\thetaθ, we should expect that this “averaging” will produce something that depends on θ\thetaθ. We’ll call our average the frequentist risk:

Bayesian loss analysis¶

In the Bayesian world, we assume that our unknown θ\thetaθ is random. Since we observe a particular dataset xxx, we’ll be a lot more interested in the randomness in θ\thetaθ than the randomness in xxx. So, we’ll condition on the particular dataset we got, and look at the average across different possibilities for the unknown parameter θ\thetaθ. We’ll call our average the Bayesian posterior risk:

ρ(x)=Eθ∣x[ℓ(δ(x),θ)]={∑θℓ(δ(x),θ)p(θ∣x)if θ discrete∫ℓ(δ(x),θ)p(θ∣x)dθif θ continuous\begin{align*} \rho(x) &= E_{\theta|x}\left[\ell(\delta(x), \theta)\right] \\ &= \begin{cases} \displaystyle \sum_\theta \ell(\delta(x), \theta) p(\theta|x) & \text{if $\theta$ discrete} \\ \displaystyle \int \ell(\delta(x), \theta) p(\theta|x) d\theta & \text{if $\theta$ continuous} \end{cases} \end{align*}ρ(x)​=Eθ∣x​[ℓ(δ(x),θ)]=⎩⎨⎧​θ∑​ℓ(δ(x),θ)p(θ∣x)∫ℓ(δ(x),θ)p(θ∣x)dθ​if θ discreteif θ continuous​​

The Bayesian risk is a function of xxx. It tells us: given that we observed a particular dataset xxx, how poorly does the procedure δ\deltaδ do, averaged over all possible values of the parameter θ\thetaθ?

Comparing frequentist and Bayesian risk¶

Operationally, both of these look kind of similar: we’re averaging the loss with respect to some conditional probability distribution. But conceptually, they’re very different: the frequentist risk fixes the parameter, and averages over all the data; the Bayesian posterior risk fixes the data, and averages over all parameters.

The variance, Ex∣θ[(δ(x)−Ex∣θ[δ(x)])2]E_{x|\theta}\Big[\big(\delta(x) - E_{x|\theta}[\delta(x)]\big)^2\Big]Ex∣θ​[(δ(x)−Ex∣θ​[δ(x)])2], answers the question: as the data varies, how far away will δ\deltaδ be from its average value? In general, if your procedure δ\deltaδ is very sensitive to variations in the data, your variance will be high.

The bias, Ex∣θ[δ(x)]−θE_{x|\theta}[\delta(x)] - \thetaEx∣θ​[δ(x)]−θ, answers the question: how far is the average value of δ\deltaδ from the true parameter θ\thetaθ? In general, if your procedure δ\deltaδ does a good job of capturing the complexity of predicting θ\thetaθ, your bias will be low.

When trying to reduce the risk (average loss), most methods try to reduce the variance and/or the bias. Many methods for estimation and prediction try to deal with the tradeoff between variance and bias: ideally we’d like both to be as small as possible, but we often need to accept a little more of one in order to make big reductions in the other.

Bayes risk¶

The two risks above are obtained by taking the expectation with respect to either the data xxx or the parameter θ\thetaθ. What if we take the expectation with respect to both? The Bayes risk is exactly that:

R(δ)=Ex,θ[ℓ(δ(x),θ)]=Eθ[R(θ)]=Ex[R(x)]\begin{align*} R(\delta) &= E_{x, \theta} [\ell(\delta(x), \theta)] \\ &= E_\theta [R(\theta)] \\ &= E_x [R(x)] \end{align*}R(δ)​=Ex,θ​[ℓ(δ(x),θ)]=Eθ​[R(θ)]=Ex​[R(x)]​
(9)

where the last two inequalities follow from Fubini’s theorem (i.e., that we can do the integrals for the expectations in either order and get the same result). The Bayes risk is a single number that summarizes the procedure δ\deltaδ. The name is somewhat misleading: it isn’t really Bayesian or frequentist.
