Many real-world data science problems boil down to making binary decisions. For example:

*   Given the outcome of medical tests for some disease on a population of patients, which patients have that disease?

*   Given the results of experimenting with a new version of my website, does that version increase the chances that a customer will stay on my website longer and/or buy something?

*   Are there “statistically significant” associations between certain pairs of variables in my dataset?

In particular, in most real-world settings, we don’t just want to analyze a single decision: we want to evaluate what happens when making multiple decisions. Toward that end, we’ll focus on frameworks we can use that help us understand the consequences of making multiple (usually related) decisions.

[PRE0]

In this chapter, we’ll focus on the case of making binary (0/1) decisions. For each decision we make, we’ll assume there’s some *Reality* or truth that’s either 0 or 1\. We gather data, and make a binary *Decision* based on the data: the decision is our best guess for reality. We’ll abbreviate Reality and Decision with <semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R</annotation></semantics>R and <semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D</annotation></semantics>D respectively. Since both are binary, we can visualize all possible outcomes using a 2 x 2 table:

|  | <semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=0</annotation></semantics>D=0 | <semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=1</annotation></semantics>D=1 |
| <semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R=0</annotation></semantics>R=0 |  |  |
| <semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R=1</annotation></semantics>R=1 |  |  |

Each decision we make must fall into one of the four cells of this table. In most real-world settings, we don’t actually know which *row* our decision lands up in: we know whether we decided <semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=0</annotation></semantics>D=0 or <semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=1</annotation></semantics>D=1 (i.e., which column it’s in), but we don’t know the state of reality.

This kind of table is often called a **confusion matrix**. There isn’t a standard convention about whether to put reality in the rows or columns, so you may see them flipped in other places. In this book, we’ll always use reality in the rows and the decision in the columns.

We use the following names for each of the four cases: **true positive** (TP), **false positive** (FP), **true negative** (TN), and **false negative** (FN).

|  | <semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=0</annotation></semantics>D=0 | <semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=1</annotation></semantics>D=1 |
| <semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R=0</annotation></semantics>R=0 | <semantics><mrow><mi>T</mi><mi>N</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">TN</annotation></semantics>TN | <semantics><mrow><mi>F</mi><mi>P</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">FP</annotation></semantics>FP |
| <semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R=1</annotation></semantics>R=1 | <semantics><mrow><mi>F</mi><mi>N</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">FN</annotation></semantics>FN | <semantics><mrow><mi>T</mi><mi>P</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">TP</annotation></semantics>TP |

The first word in each name tells us whether or not the decision was correct (“true”) or incorrect (“false”). The second word tells us whether the decision was a 1 (“positive”) or 0 (“negative”). For example, a “false positive” is a decision where <semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=1</annotation></semantics>D=1 (because it was a ‘positive’) and was incorrect, so <semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R=0</annotation></semantics>R=0 (because it was ‘false’).

Ideally, our decisions would always match reality, and land in the top left (true negatives) or bottom right (false positives) cells of our table. In the real world, this is not always possible: we may make errors. Depending on the problem, we may prefer to avoid one kind of error more than the other. In the rest of this section, we’ll define several different ways to quantify the errors we make, and understand the relationship between them.

There’s a lot of rich analysis we can do on a single decision, which we’ll explore in the next section on hypothesis testing. For now, we’ll start with the case of making multiple decisions, which will be our focus for most of this chapter.

## Making multiple decisions[¶](#making-multiple-decisions "Link to this Section")

Suppose now that we make multiple decisions, and we want to look at all of them collectively. There are several ways this situation could arise:

*Testing multiple hypotheses on a single dataset*: we could be interested in asking multiple questions from a single dataset. For example, suppose we examine a genomics dataset in an Alzheimer’s study. For each of thousands of genetic markers, we would like to ask whether a mutation at that location is associated with a higher risk of Alzheimer’s.

*Testing one hypothesis on multiple datasets*: suppose we’re interested in studying whether there is an association between the use of statin drugs and the rate of heart attacks. We could conduct a meta-analysis of many studies that look at this question: each study tests the same hypothesis (i.e., is taking statin drugs associated with lower risk of heart attack?) with a different set of subjects. By looking at the studies as a collection, we can try to draw a stronger conclusion about the presence (or absence) of such a link.

### Modifying the 2x2 table[¶](#modifying-the-2x2-table "Link to this Section")

When we make multiple decisions, we may have multiple false positives, true negatives, and so on. So, we’ll use the same 2x2 table, but now each cell will contain a count of how many times that condition happened:

|  | <semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=0</annotation></semantics>D=0 | <semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=1</annotation></semantics>D=1 |
| <semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R=0</annotation></semantics>R=0 | <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>00</mn></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">n_{00}</annotation></semantics>n00​ | <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>01</mn></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">n_{01}</annotation></semantics>n01​ |
| <semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R=1</annotation></semantics>R=1 | <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>10</mn></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">n_{10}</annotation></semantics>n10​ | <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>11</mn></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">n_{11}</annotation></semantics>n11​ |

For example, <semantics><mrow><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>01</mn></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">n_{01}</annotation></semantics>n01​ is the number of times reality was 0 and we made a decision of 1.

There are many ways to quantify how well (or how badly) our decisions match up with reality. In this book, we’ll focus on **row-wise rates**, which quantify our performance in each row (i.e., when reality is 0 and when reality is 1), and **column-wise rates**, which quantify our performance in each column (i.e., when the decision is 0 or the decision is 1).

### Row-wise rates: quantifying how well we do when reality is known[¶](#row-wise-rates-quantifying-how-well-we-do-when-reality-is-known "Link to this Section")

Suppose we want to evaluate how good our decision-making process is in cases where reality is 0\. In this case, we’re interested in the top row of the 2x2 table above. In particular, we can quantify how often we’re correct in this situation using the fraction <semantics><mrow><mfrac><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>00</mn></msub><mrow><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>00</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>01</mn></msub></mrow></mfrac></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\frac{n_{00}}{n_{00} + n_{01}}</annotation></semantics>n00​+n01​n00​​. We’ll call this row-wise rate the **true negative rate** (TNR): it quantifies how often we make true negative decisions in cases where reality is 0.

We can also evaluate our decision-making process in cases where reality is 1\. In this case, we can quantify how often we’re correct using <semantics><mrow><mfrac><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>11</mn></msub><mrow><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>10</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>11</mn></msub></mrow></mfrac></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\frac{n_{11}}{n_{10} + n_{11}}</annotation></semantics>n10​+n11​n11​​. This is called the **true positive rate** (TPR): it measures how often we make true positive decisions in cases where reality is 1.

We can define corresponding **error rates** for the two cases above, which we’ll call false positive rate (FPR) and false negative rate (FNR):

<semantics><mrow><mi>F</mi><mi>P</mi><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mfrac><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>01</mn></msub><mrow><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>00</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>01</mn></msub></mrow></mfrac><mi>F</mi><mi>N</mi><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mfrac><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>10</mn></msub><mrow><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>10</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>11</mn></msub></mrow></mfrac></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">FPR = \frac{n_{01}}{n_{00} + n_{01}} \\ FNR = \frac{n_{10}}{n_{10} + n_{11}}</annotation></semantics>FPR=n00​+n01​n01​​FNR=n10​+n11​n10​​[(1)](#p7PBp5CxTW "Link to this Equation")

We can visualize these rates with the rows that they correspond to in our 2x2 table:

![row_wise_rates.png](../Images/09718410b67d25c8e8a19676bb1db944.png)

As an example, suppose we’re predicting whether or not a customer will buy a product. In this case, 0 corresponds to a customer not buying, and 1 corresponds to the customer buying. Here’s how we’d interpret the four rates defined above:

*   TNR: when the customer **did not** buy the product, how often did we (correctly) predict that they would not?

*   FPR: when the customer **did not** buy the product, how often did we (incorrectly) predict that they would buy it?

*   TPR: when the customer **did** buy the product, how often did we (correctly) predict that they would buy it?

*   FNR: when the customer **did** buy the product, how often did we (incorrectly) predict that they would not?

### Column-wise rates[¶](#column-wise-rates "Link to this Section")

Row-wise rates measure how well we do in each case of reality: in other words, in each row of the table. We can also quantify our performance when we make specific decisions. We’ll focus on the **false discovery proportion** (FDP), which quantifies how often we’re wrong when we make a decision of 1:

![fdp.png](../Images/b70a4f6f3194c6f53e7bc0a1196e5d0e.png)

We also define the **false omission proportion** (FOP), which quantifies how often we’re wrong when we make a decision of 0:

<semantics><mrow><mi>F</mi><mi>O</mi><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><mfrac><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>10</mn></msub><mrow><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>00</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>10</mn></msub></mrow></mfrac></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">FOP = \frac{n_{10}}{n_{00} + n_{10}}</annotation></semantics>FOP=n00​+n10​n10​​[(2)](#i1G4dDsI7k "Link to this Equation")

For each of these two, we can define their opposites (true omission proportion and true discovery proportion), but these are rarely called by these names.

Returning to our product purchase prediction example, here’s how we’d interpret the column-wise rates:

*   FDP: when we predicted the customer **would buy** the product, how often did they end up not buying it?

*   FOP: when we predicted the customer **would not buy** the product, how often did they end up buying it?

Throughout this book, we’ll focus primarily on FDP as the column-wise rate we’re most interested in.

#### Precision[¶](#precision "Link to this Section")

A closely related and widely used column-wise rate is the **precision**:

<semantics><mrow><mtext>Precision</mtext><mo>=</mo><mfrac><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>11</mn></msub><mrow><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>01</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><msub><mi>n</mi><mn>11</mn></msub></mrow></mfrac><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn><mo>−</mo><mi>F</mi><mi>D</mi><mi>P</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\text{Precision} = \frac{n_{11}}{n_{01} + n_{11}} = 1 - FDP</annotation></semantics>Precision=n01​+n11​n11​​=1−FDP[(3)](#nEBckzrgvY "Link to this Equation")

Precision tells us out of the times we made a decision of 1, how often we were correct. In the product purchase prediction example, we’d interpret it as: when we predicted the customer **would buy** the product, how often did they end up buying it?

### Example: interpreting row-wise and column-wise rates[¶](#example-interpreting-row-wise-and-column-wise-rates "Link to this Section")

Suppose we’re testing a large number of patients for a disease. We’ll use 1 to indicate that a patient has the disease, and 0 to indicate that they don’t. This corresponds to standard medical terminology, where a “positive” test indicates that we think someone has the disease, while a “negative” test means we think they don’t have it.

Let’s interpret some of the definitions we’ve learned for this specific example. Recall that for this example, a **false positive** occurs when the test comes back positive (i.e., the test says the patient has the disease), but the patient is healthy.

*   The false positive rate (FPR) is the percentage of healthy patients that test positive. It tells us: **when we test healthy patients, how often is the test wrong?**

*   The false negative rate (FNR) is the percentage of sick patients that test negative. It tells us: **when we test sick patients, how often is the test wrong?**

*   The false discovery proportion (FDP) is the percentage of positive tests that are from healthy patients. It tells us: **when the test results are positive, how often are they wrong?**

*   The false omission proportion (FOP) is the percentage of negative tests that are from sick patients. It tells us: **when the test results are negative, how often are they wrong?**

Intuitively, the row-wise error rates (FPR and FNR) describe properties of the test itself: they tell us how well the test does on healthy patients and sick patients, respectively. As a specific example, the COVID-19 rapid (antigen) tests have an estimated FPR of 0.2% and an FNR of 38% for the Omicron BA.1 variant ([source](https://doi.org/10.1101/2022.06.13.22276325)). The FPR and FNR are a property of the testing technique and the disease itself, and are the same regardless of whether the test is used during a surge in cases or during a low period (fewer cases), as long as the test technology and variant are the same.

On the other hand, the column-wise rates must depend on the population. As a specific example, let’s examine the FDP for COVID-19 rapid (antigen) tests. During a lull, when the virus is less common in the population, we should expect a relatively small number of people with COVID, and a relatively large number of healthy patients. This means that some moderate percentage of positive tests will be incorrect (false positives), and so our FDR could be moderately high. But during a surge in cases, many more people are sick, and we should expect more of the positive tests to come from people with COVID rather than healthy people. Therefore, our FDP will be lower, even if the test itself stays exactly the same.

Next, we’ll quantify this intuition for the relationship between row-wise rates and column-wise rates.

### Conditional probabilities[¶](#conditional-probabilities "Link to this Section")

Each of the rates defined above was based on the idea of limiting ourselves to half of the table. In the case of row-wise rates, we defined quantities based on looking only at <semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R=0</annotation></semantics>R=0 (FPR and TNR) or <semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R=1</annotation></semantics>R=1 (TPR and FNR). In the case of column-wise rates, we defined FDP based only on <semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=1</annotation></semantics>D=1.

This idea, of making computations based on a limited set of outcomes, should be familiar: it’s an idea you’ve likely seen before when thinking about conditional probabilities. Consider events <semantics><mrow><mi>A</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">A</annotation></semantics>A and <semantics><mrow><mi>B</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">B</annotation></semantics>B. While <semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>A</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P(A)</annotation></semantics>P(A) describes the probability that event <semantics><mrow><mi>A</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">A</annotation></semantics>A occurs, <semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>A</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>B</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P(A|B)</annotation></semantics>P(A∣B) describes the conditional probability of event <semantics><mrow><mi>A</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">A</annotation></semantics>A given event <semantics><mrow><mi>B</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">B</annotation></semantics>B: in other words, if we restrict ourselves to the case where event <semantics><mrow><mi>B</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">B</annotation></semantics>B has occurred, what is the probability of event <semantics><mrow><mi>A</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">A</annotation></semantics>A occuring?

From this idea, we can interpret our row-wise rates as conditional probabilities. Since each is defined within a particular case for reality, we know that they are probabilities of particular decisions conditioned on reality: for example, the true positive rate (TPR) is <semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P(D=1|R=1)</annotation></semantics>P(D=1∣R=1).

Similarly, the false positive rate (FPR), which measures how often our answers are incorrect (<semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=1</annotation></semantics>D=1) when reality is 0 (<semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R = 0</annotation></semantics>R=0), can be interpreted as the conditional probability <semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P(D=1|R=0)</annotation></semantics>P(D=1∣R=0).

Column-wise rates, on the other hand, are defined conditioned on a particular decision. The false discovery proportion, which looks at our positive decisions (<semantics><mrow><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">D=1</annotation></semantics>D=1) and measures how often they are incorrect (<semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R=0</annotation></semantics>R=0), can be interpreted as the conditional probability <semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P(R=0|D=1)</annotation></semantics>P(R=0∣D=1). Similarly, the false omission proportion (FOP) is <semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn><mi mathvariant="normal">∣</mi><mi>D</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P(R=1|D=0)</annotation></semantics>P(R=1∣D=0).

Interpreting these row-wise and column-wise rates as conditional probabilities is useful because it lets us apply everything we know about conditional probability to better our understanding of these rates. Earlier, with our COVID-19 testing example, we explored an intuitive connection between the row-wise rates and column-wise rates, and introduced the idea that the connection also depends on how common “<semantics><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">R=1</annotation></semantics>R=1” is. We can formalize this intuition by applying Bayes’ rule.

### Relating row-wise and column-wise error rates[¶](#relating-row-wise-and-column-wise-error-rates "Link to this Section")

### Different error types: is one worse than the other?[¶](#different-error-types-is-one-worse-than-the-other "Link to this Section")

False positives and false negatives are both errors. In general, we’d like to make as few errors as possible, but as we’ll see later, we often end up in situations where we must trade off between making more false positives and fewer false negatives, or vice versa. In such cases, it’s important to understand which one is preferable (or “less bad”).

For example: suppose Spotify is trying to predict which customers will renew their subscriptions (1 corresponds to renewing, 0 corresponds to not renewing). For customers predicted to not renew, they plan to send a $1 coupon off their next month’s subscription. In this case:

*   A false positive corresponds to predicting a customer will renew (and therefore not sending a coupon), but then the customer doesn’t renew (and Spotify loses a customer paying $12 per month, every month).

*   A false negative corresponds to predicting a customer won’t renew (and therefore sending them a coupon), but then the customer would have renewed anyway (and so the coupon was unnecessary, costing Spotify $1).

In this example, we could probably argue that a false positive is worse than a false negative: a false negative means that Spotify will waste $1, but a false positive means that they will lose a paying customer who they might have been able to retain with a coupon.
